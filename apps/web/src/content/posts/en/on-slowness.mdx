Speed is not the problem. The problem is when speed becomes the only
measure we have, and everything that only shows itself slowly stops
being visible at all — the real rhythm of a sentence, the real shape of
a program, the thing a person actually meant to say.

## Attention is finite

You can spend attention, but you cannot borrow it. A morning of
interruptions does not cost you a morning. It costs you the afternoon
you would have needed to recover the thread.

The arithmetic is unforgiving:

- four hours of interrupted work is not four hours
- a context switch costs more than the switch itself
- the residue of "I should check that" outlives the check

## What slow work looks like

Slow work is not work done slowly. It is work where the pace is set by
the material rather than by the calendar. A sentence that needs three
passes gets three passes.

> The work is not finished when there is nothing left to add, but when
> there is nothing left to take away.

In practice that mostly means removing things. Deleting a function is
usually better than documenting it. Deleting the abstraction is usually
better than generalising it.

## A note on tooling

The same holds for the tools we build. A format that stays readable
after ten years is worth more than one that saves keystrokes today. If
your build step needs a paragraph of explanation to justify itself, that
paragraph is the bug.

You can measure this the boring way: `git log --diff-filter=D`, or by
counting how much of last year's code you can still explain line by
line.

The desk is a good metaphor for all of it. Nothing on it is fast. Paper
does not refresh. But you can see everything at once, and you can put
it down.
